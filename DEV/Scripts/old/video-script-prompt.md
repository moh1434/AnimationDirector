# Writing for Benny and Berry

Write short, voice-led cartoons about a present-day family. The pleasure is in recognizing something people do with those closest to them. Start with one person's experience. Give the person across from them a life of their own.

Read [the family bible](../logical-fallacies/FAMILY_BIBLE.md) for the cast and relationships. For the continuing series, read [the episode rules](../logical-fallacies/RULES.md) and [approved history](../logical-fallacies/SERIES_LOG.md). The episode rules govern its file format and review process.

## Finding the story

Think of a moment that could happen this week. A parent gives away a jacket that still matters to a child. Two people both need ten minutes alone after coming home. A sister tells a funny story at dinner and discovers it was private. Pick the moment because somebody stands to lose something they value. Everyday stakes can be quite small and still hurt.

Before pitching, answer these questions in ordinary language:

- Whose day are we following? What did they expect to happen today?
- Why did the other person do what they did? Could they tell their side convincingly?
- What do these two usually do together that makes today feel different?
- What choice has an effect the viewer can see, and who carries it into the next scene?

Use a specific detail from their life together. A father putting his daughter's shoes by the door before she asks tells us he remembers their plans. Finding the shoes there that night can tell us he missed them. Let the viewer make that connection. Aim for the same specificity when the mood is playful or affectionate. A good day together can carry a story too.

## Scenes and pace

Open on the first interesting change in a familiar routine. A second place setting quietly going back in the cupboard can raise a question before anyone speaks. Begin close enough to that change that we can see what the first remark means.

Stay for the reaction. Someone may keep folding a towel because answering their partner would mean admitting they forgot. If that silence changes how we understand the next line, give it space on the page and in the timing estimate.

Cut when something has changed across the interval. An awkward breakfast can cut to a quiet walk home, where the person who said yes has had all day to reconsider. Establish the new moment with light, clothes, an object or the activity in progress. A single unbroken scene also works when the decision happens right there. Use close-ups for details that the viewpoint character notices.

End on the consequence we came to see. A revised plan, a small refusal, a shared laugh or an offer accepted a little reluctantly can finish the episode. Humour can live anywhere. Draw it from how these people know each other and leave room for a tender or uncomfortable ending.

## Writing for voice and picture

- Use character dialogue and visible action. The show has no narrator. Keep the exchange easy to follow by ear. Give the listener room to register a change of feeling.
- Write one breath per line, usually 4–14 words and always under 200 characters. Allow short answers and unfinished thoughts. Speakers take turns. Spell numbers as spoken. Keep performance directions outside spoken text.
- Favour one or two familiar settings, a few speakers and simple actions readable on a phone. A look at an empty seat is easier to stage than a close-up of a long message. Avoid relying on legible screens, crowds, logos or intricate hand movements.
- At script stage, establish language and any requested runtime. For a bilingual episode write English first, then natural spoken Arabic with the same speakers, lines and order. Choose humour that travels. Keep brand names in Latin letters when supplied. Avoid semicolons in Arabic dialogue. Leave production notes outside both voice tracks.
- Record factual claims that need checking under `# FACTS TO CHECK`. Keep invented family incidents distinct from claims about the real world.

### Estimating length

Use the project's measured voice speeds in words per second. Use the fallback for Wendy and other unmeasured voices.

| Voice | English | Arabic |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Benny | 2.42 | 1.75 |
| Berry | 2.92 | 1.85 |
| Cherry | 2.89 | 1.95 |
| Fallback | 2.4 | 1.8 |

Add each speaker's word count divided by their speed. Allow roughly 0.25 seconds per line for ordinary gaps. For a longer reaction, replace that gap with the intended hold. Add wordless opening or ending time separately. Estimate each language independently. If the scene feels rushed, try removing a spoken explanation and listening through the intended silence. Final recordings determine timing.
