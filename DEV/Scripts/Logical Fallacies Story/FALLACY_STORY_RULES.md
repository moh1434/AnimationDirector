# Logical Fallacies Story: rules for writing an episode

You turn a short idea into a finished, voice-ready script for a cartoon series that helps viewers
recognize bad reasoning. Each script becomes a vertical video of 45 seconds to 3 minutes: the lines
are voiced, then an animator draws the picture to fit the voices.

## 0. Read these first

1. **`../video-script-prompt.md`**, the Berry and Benny series rules. Its sections on the cast (1),
   the hook (3), what the cartoon can show (6), writing for the voice (7), facts (8) and two
   languages (11) apply here unchanged. **Where this file says something different, this file wins.**
   If you were given only this file, ask for that one before writing.
2. **`SERIES_LOG.md`** (this folder): what has already happened in the series, what each
   character has learned, and which setups are still waiting for a payoff. Never contradict it.

What changes from `video-script-prompt.md`: the episode shape (section 3 here replaces its formats
A to D), lengths up to 3 minutes (section 8), and the output header (section 9).
What stays: **no narrator**, ever. Everything is told through what the characters say and do.

## 1. The series in one sentence

Someone reasons badly, acts on it, and somebody notices. The viewer laughs and learns to notice too.

- **For a general audience**, kids and adults together.
- **The setting may be fantastical** if the idea needs that (kings, treasure, magic, talking animals, a village with a dragon
  problem). **The reasoning must feel familiar**: the kind people use with a friend, a business
  partner, a family, a group chat or a town meeting.
- **A story first, a lesson second.** If the episode would still work with the lesson cut out, good.
  If it is only a lesson in costume, rewrite it.

## 2. From the idea to the plan

The idea might be a situation, a fallacy, a moral or a setting. Before writing any line, work out:


| Plan item                | What it means                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The want**             | What a character wants, or the problem they must solve. Concrete: the valuable item, a fair split, winning an argument, winning the bake-off. |
| **The bad step**         | The exact sentence where the reasoning goes wrong.                                                                                            |
| **The missing piece**    | The fact, question or test that shows it is wrong. It must reach the viewer on screen or in dialogue.                                         |
| **The real-life mirror** | Where the viewer has met this reasoning in their own life.                                                                                    |
| **The fallacy**          | Its name, for the`#` notes only. The appendix lists common ones.                                                                              |

**The bad step must sound reasonable.** Someone smart and likeable says it with confidence, and a
viewer should half-agree the first time they hear it. If nobody would ever believe it, it is a
strawman: rewrite it until it is tempting.

If the idea names a fallacy, use it. If the reasoning in the idea is actually sound, or the flaw is
unclear, say so and suggest the closest fallacy before writing.

**Ask before writing only if the idea is not clear. The idea could contain one or more logical fallacies,
or the idea contradicts `SERIES_LOG.md`. Otherwise, write. If you cannot ask,
choose, and record the choice in a `#` note at the top.

## 3. The episode shape


| Beat               | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1.**Hook**         | Within the first 4 seconds (one or two lines), the viewer has a question they want answered: what someone wants, a problem already going wrong, or a strange claim said with confidence. Keep the first line short enough to say in one breath (aim for about 12 words or fewer), and let the first picture show what the words don't. Write three hooks and let the user pick one.
| 2.**The bad step** | Said with confidence by someone the audience likes.                                                                                                                                                  |
| 3.**Acting on it** | They commit. It seems to work, or it grows. Most of the crux live here: follow the bad rule all the way to its absurd conclusion.                                                                    |
| 4.**The catch**    | Someone notices, in a way that fits the scene (section 4).                                                                                                                                           |
| 5.**The landing**  | What really happened, or what they do instead. The want is won, lost or twisted. One character says**the takeaway** (below).                                                                         |
| 6.**The button**   | One last line: a joke, ideally a callback to something earlier. It comes after the takeaway, so the episode ends on a laugh, not a lesson. Then stop.                                                |

**Several fallacies in one episode** are allowed when the idea needs them. Each one gets its own
bad step, action and catch (beats 2 to 4), and each must be a different fallacy, or the same one
growing bigger, never the same idea twice. Escalate, like three tries to convince the same stubborn
person. Each one needs room to land, so as a rough guide: about one per 20–30 seconds, and up to
3 in an episode unless the user asks for more.

**The takeaway.** Once per fallacy, after its catch, one character says the general rule in everyday
words: a sentence the viewer can reuse the next time they hear the same reasoning. After a false cause,
for example: "Just because two things happen one after another doesn't mean one caused the other."

- One sentence, about 15 words or fewer, spoken in character as part of the scene (a reply, a
  realisation), never a speech to the camera.
- Best from the one who was wrong, as they realise it, or from Wendy. Vary who says it.
- Never the fallacy's name, and never "I learned that...", "The lesson is..." or "The moral is...".
- It confirms what the viewer has already seen in the catch; it doesn't explain it. If the episode
  only makes sense once the takeaway is said, the catch is not clear enough: fix the catch.
- With several fallacies, each gets its own takeaway, right after its own catch.

## 4. The catch

The catch hands the viewer the missing piece. It never gives a lecture. Choose the kind that suits
the character and the scene:


| Kind                                  | Example                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The awkward question**              | "What were his exact words?"                                                         |
| **Check what actually happened**      | Count the coins. Open the ledger. Go and ask the king.                               |
| **Run the test**                      | Try it again, changing one thing.                                                    |
| **The counterexample in plain sight** | Something already on screen breaks the rule (planted earlier).                       |
| **The self-catch**                    | The one who was wrong hears themselves: "...Did I just say fire trucks cause fires?" |
| **Same logic, sillier case**          | "My cousin got an umbrella and then it rained. Ban umbrellas?"                       |
| **The consequence answers**           | What happens next proves it, with no one needing to say anything.                    |

Rules for the catch:

- **The catch is specific; the takeaway is general.** The catch works on this situation: a question,
  a check, a test. The general rule comes once, afterwards, as the takeaway (section 3). Neither one
  explains the flaw at length.
- **Play fair.** Anything the catch depends on should be seen or heard before the catch, so a viewer
  who rewinds can find it.
- **A bad argument does not make its conclusion false.** Catching the flaw is not proof of the
  opposite. Sam's reason for refusing the fire truck is bad, but that alone doesn't prove the
  village needs one; the mayor's evidence does. Don't let the catcher make a new mistake, unless
  that is the joke and someone notices.
- **Don't teach cynicism.** The lesson is about one step of reasoning, not "trust nobody". Trust is a
  good reason to believe someone; it is not a reason to skip a check that is cheap when the
  stakes are high.

## 5. Humor

- **Take the bad logic seriously.** The characters act on it fully and confidently, and the crux
  comes from following it.
- **Specific beats general.** "He found a coin in the fountain and gave it back. Twice." is funnier
  and more convincing than "He's always honest."
- **Use threes, callbacks and the button.**
- The one who was wrong keeps their dignity and stays likeable: viewers should recognize themselves in them.

## 6. Characters

- **Berry (kid, about 10), Cherry (teen, about 15), Wendy (about 18) and Benny (about 20)** are as
  described in `video-script-prompt.md` section 1. **Benny makes most of the bad steps, and Wendy
  catches most of them**: as a guide, Benny reasons badly in about two episodes out of three and Wendy
  makes about two catches out of three. The rest keeps the series from becoming predictable: Berry or
  Cherry falls for it, Berry, Cherry or even Benny catches it, and once in a while Wendy is the one who
  is wrong. Their ages give each a natural kind of mistake and a natural kind of catch:


  |        | Tends to fall for                                                                                                 | Tends to catch it by                                                                        |
  | ------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | Berry  | jumping to conclusions, after-this-so-because-of-this, one example for everyone                                   | a blunt "Why?", trying it right away                                                        |
  | Cherry | trusting nobody after being fooled, "everyone at school says so"                                                  | counting, checking the receipts and the record                                              |
  | Wendy  | trusting her own tests and plans too much (rarely)                                                                | most catches: a question, checking what actually happened, or a small test everyone watches |
  | Benny  | most mistakes: trusting a friendly face or a good story, one lucky day as a rule, deciding first, sunk cost, pity | now and then, a strange question that breaks the argument by accident                       |

  The ages also choose the stakes: pocket money and school for Berry, fairness and reputation for
  Cherry, a release or a playtest for Wendy, the juice stand's money and partners for Benny.
- **Side characters**: reuse the ones in `SERIES_LOG.md` or create new ones (a king, a mayor, a
  merchant, a stubborn villager). Give each a short lower-case id and one clear want.
- **Benny is the one who usually reasons badly.** Keep him likeable: his bad step must sound
  reasonable, he says it with full confidence, and he takes being caught with good humour. He learns:
  he doesn't fall for the same fallacy in the same way twice (check `SERIES_LOG.md`), so give him a new
  mistake each time.
- **Wendy is the wise character and the usual catcher.** She catches the flaw the way she fixes a bug:
  one question, a look at what actually happened, or a small test the others watch. She never lectures
  and never names the fallacy. She speaks less than the others, so her one line lands. Wise is not the
  same as never wrong: now and then an episode catches her out. Don't invent another recurring mentor.
- **Placeholders** in the user's drafts ("Character A") are cast from the trio or the side characters.
- **Speakers:** three or fewer when possible. A town-sized story (a trial, a meeting) may use four or
  five. Silent characters cost nothing.
- **Characters remember.** Someone who learned a lesson on screen doesn't fall for the same
  fallacy in the same way. They may fall for its mirror image instead: fooled once, now they trust
  nobody, which is its own flaw and makes a good payoff.

## 7. Standalone episodes and arcs

Mark every episode as one of these:

- **Standalone**: complete by itself.
- **Setup**: a trip, time to study or learn a skill, someone earning the group's trust, a promise, a
  rule someone makes. It still needs its own hook, jokes and small payoff. It may contain a smaller
  fallacy or none, but it must **plant** something concrete and drawable: an object, a skill, a
  promise, a record ("Benny gave the coin back. Twice.").
- **Payoff**: a problem that tests what was planted: what someone learned or believed, or the trust
  they earned. Give a new viewer a one-line reminder inside natural dialogue, never "Remember when...".

Every plant goes into the `# LOG UPDATE` block (section 9). A plant doesn't have to pay off soon,
but it must never be forgotten or contradicted.

Arc shapes that suit the series:

- Trust earned over several episodes, then tested.
- A skill learned (reading the shop's accounts), later used to catch a dishonest partner.
- A trip to a town with an odd tradition, which comes back when someone at home says "we've always
  done it this way".
- A character fooled once who overcorrects next time.

## 8. Length

Use the voice speeds and the exact-estimate formula in `video-script-prompt.md` section 5. Its quick
table, extended to 3 minutes:


| Target  | English words | Arabic words | Lines  |
| ------- | ------------- | ------------ | ------ |
| 30 s    | 70–80        | 46–55       | 8–12  |
| 45 s    | 105–120      | 70–82       | 12–16 |
| 60 s    | 140–160      | 95–110      | 16–22 |
| 90 s    | 210–240      | 140–165     | 24–32 |

| 2½ min | 350–400      | 240–275     | 40–50 |
| 3 min   | 420–480      | 285–330     | 48–60 |

Shorter is better when the idea allows it. Never pad. Past about 45 seconds, open a fresh small
question every 15–25 seconds. An idea that needs more than 3 minutes becomes two episodes.
Several episodes in one file follow the layout of `../05-lost-things-returned-here-series.txt`
(a `# SERIES` header, then `# EPISODE 1`, `# EPISODE 2` and so on).

## 9. Output

First the three hook options, numbered, each with one line on the question it opens, and ask the user
to pick one. Write the script after they choose; if the user can't be asked, pick the best one and say
why in one line. Then the script in exactly this form:

```text
# TITLE: (a story title, never the fallacy's name)
# ARC: standalone | setup for <what is planted> | payoff of <what was planted>
# LENGTH: about N s, N spoken words
# WANT: what the characters want, or the problem
# FALLACY 1: <name> | <the bad step, in one line>
# TAKEAWAY 1: <who says it> | <the takeaway line>
# REAL LIFE: where the viewer has met this reasoning
# CAST: ids
# PLACE: place, time of day

# SEE: what is on screen, in one short sentence.
id: spoken words
```

- Every line starting with `#` is a note. It is never voiced, captioned or translated.
- Every other line is `id: spoken words`, with a lower-case id. Never `narrator`.
- One `# SEE:` note before each beat.
- Straight after each catch, mark it: `#NOTE_[Fallacy 1: <name>]`. With several fallacies,
  number them in order.
- After the script, add `# FACTS TO CHECK` if the episode states any real-world fact.
- Last comes `# LOG UPDATE`: the lines to add to `SERIES_LOG.md` if the episode is approved (what each
  character now knows or believes, new side characters, what was planted or paid off, new running
  jokes). Don't edit `SERIES_LOG.md` yourself unless you're asked to.

## 10. Check before you answer

Fix the script until every answer is yes:

- Within the first two lines, does the viewer have a question (a want, a problem or a strange claim),
  and is the first line short enough to say in one breath?
- Would a viewer half-believe the bad step when they first hear it?
- Can the viewer see where the reasoning went wrong from the catch alone, before the takeaway is said?
- Does each catch fit the character and the scene: a question, a check, a test, a self-catch?
- Is the fallacy's name kept out of every spoken line, the title and every object on screen?
- Is there one takeaway per fallacy: one everyday sentence, said in character after its catch, with no
  fallacy name and no "I learned"?
- Does the episode end on the button (a joke), not on the takeaway? Is there no narrator?
- Does the person who was wrong stay likeable?
- Is it funny: at least one real laugh per beat in the middle?
- Is every line needed, and is the word count inside the range?
- Is every beat drawable (`video-script-prompt.md` section 6), and is everything consistent with `SERIES_LOG.md`?

## Example

This is the user's "Trusted Friend" draft rewritten in this form. When writing a new episode,
don't reuse its lines.

```text
# HOOK OPTIONS
# 1. The king said I get the crown.
# 2. Why is Benny wearing the king's crown?
# 3. Benny never lies. That's the problem.
# CHOSEN (by the user): 1, a confident claim the viewer instantly wants checked, and the crown is on screen.

# TITLE: The King Said So
# ARC: payoff of Benny's honest reputation
# LENGTH: about 57 s, 133 spoken words
# WANT: the friends found treasure and must decide who gets the best pieces
# FALLACY 1: Past honesty treated as proof (a kind of appeal to authority) | "If Benny says the king said it, the king said it."
# TAKEAWAY 1: wendy | "Honest people can still tell a story wrong."
# REAL LIFE: believing a reliable friend's summary of what the boss said, instead of the boss's own words
# CAST: benny, berry, wendy, king
# PLACE: ruins, day

# SEE: Benny sits on a pile of treasure in the ruins, wearing a crown. Berry and Wendy walk in.
benny: The king said I get the crown.
berry: He did?
benny: And the gem. He said it's my reward.

# SEE: Berry looks at Wendy. Wendy shrugs.
berry: Well, Benny never lies.
berry: He found a coin in the fountain and gave it back. Twice.
wendy: He told me he ate my sandwich before I even noticed.
benny: It was a very good sandwich.
berry: So if Benny says the king said it, the king said it.

# SEE: Benny pulls a golden cup, a shield and a lamp onto his pile.
benny: Oh, and the cup. And the shield. And the lamp.
berry: Of course.

# SEE: Wendy picks up the lamp, turns it over, and looks at Benny.
wendy: Benny... what were the king's exact words?
benny: His exact words were... Benny, keep an eye on the treasure.
# SEE: Silence. Berry stares. Benny straightens the crown.
berry: Keep an eye on it.
benny: I'm keeping two eyes on it.
#NOTE_[Fallacy 1: Past honesty treated as proof]
wendy: Honest people can still tell a story wrong. Next time, we ask the king.

# SEE: The king is standing behind them, arms crossed.
king: Ask the king what?
# SEE: Benny slowly takes off the crown and holds it out.
benny: Your Majesty. I was keeping an eye on it.
king: With your head?

# LOG UPDATE
# - Benny: honest reputation (the fountain coin, twice; confessed to eating Wendy's sandwich). Stretched the king's words once, got caught.
# - Berry: learned to check the source's own words, not a friend's summary.
# - king (id king): rules the land; asked Benny to watch the treasure from the ruins.
# - Running joke: "I'm keeping an eye on it."
```

## Appendix: common fallacies

The name is for `#` notes only. "Sounds like" is how it might appear in a story; "Natural catch" is one
way a character can notice it.


| Fallacy                                                        | Sounds like                                                            | Natural catch                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| False cause (after this, so because of this)                   | "He got a fire truck, then his house burned. Fire trucks bring fires." | Same logic, sillier case; or check the order of events.      |
| Ad hominem (attack the person)                                 | "You can't even fix the road, so you're wrong about the truck."        | "Okay. And the fire truck?"                                  |
| Appeal to tradition                                            | "We've never had one and we've been fine."                             | Something from the past that everyone now agrees was bad.    |
| Appeal to authority (unchecked)                                | "The king said so."                                                    | Ask the authority, or ask for their exact words.             |
| Past record treated as proof                                   | "He's always been honest, so this is true."                            | A cheap check of this particular claim.                      |
| Halo effect (a bias: good at one thing, so good at everything) | "He's great in the garden, so he'll be a great business partner."      | "Who's counting the money?"                                  |
| Hasty generalization                                           | "One merchant cheated me. All merchants cheat."                        | The next merchant gives back too much change.                |
| Anecdote over evidence                                         | "My grandpa never wore a helmet and he was fine."                      | "How many grandpas weren't fine?"                            |
| Survivorship bias                                              | "Every famous baker skipped school."                                   | Ask about the ones who skipped school and didn't get famous. |
| Cherry picking                                                 | Showing only the good days of the lemonade stand.                      | Open the full ledger.                                        |
| Bandwagon                                                      | "Everyone in town is buying moon rocks."                               | "Has anyone checked what they actually do?"                  |
| Appeal to emotion or pity                                      | "If you loved the village, you'd vote yes."                            | "I do love the village. That's why I'm asking."              |
| False dilemma                                                  | "Either we buy the dragon or the castle burns."                        | Someone names a third option.                                |
| Slippery slope                                                 | "If we let Benny have one cookie, soon he'll eat the whole bakery."    | Follow the chain until it's absurd.                          |
| Strawman                                                       | "So you want the village to burn?"                                     | "No. I said the truck is too big for the bridge."            |
| Red herring                                                    | Changing the subject to the broken road.                               | Bring it back to the question.                               |
| Tu quoque (you do it too)                                      | "You were late last week, so I can be late."                           | "True. Are we both late now?"                                |
| Sunk cost                                                      | "We've dug for three days, we can't stop now."                         | "Is the treasure here?"                                      |
| Gambler's fallacy                                              | "Tails five times. Heads is due!"                                      | The coin doesn't know. Flip it again.                        |
| Circular reasoning                                             | "The map is right because the map says so."                            | "Who drew the map?"                                          |
| Moving the goalposts                                           | "Fine, it works on Tuesday. But does it work in the rain?"             | List the tests already passed.                               |
| Appeal to ignorance                                            | "Nobody proved there's no dragon, so there is one."                    | "Nobody proved there's no giant snail either."               |
| Burden of proof shifted                                        | "Prove my potion doesn't work."                                        | "You're selling it. You prove it."                           |
| Appeal to nature                                               | "It's from the forest, so it must be healthy."                         | A forest mushroom that definitely isn't.                     |
| Composition                                                    | "Every player is great, so the team will be great."                    | They have never played together.                             |
| Loaded question                                                | "Have you stopped stealing lemons?"                                    | "I never started."                                           |
| No true Scotsman                                               | "No real knight would be scared of mice."                              | The bravest knight sees a mouse.                             |
